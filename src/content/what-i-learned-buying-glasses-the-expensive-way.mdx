<p>I knew I needed glasses for a long time before I finally did anything about it. I just kept putting it off and putting it off. I don't like going to doctors, I could still see well enough to function, and I simply didn't want to deal with it.</p>

<p>About three years ago, I finally broke down and got my first eye exam. I think the exam cost around $129 because I didn't have vision insurance—or optical insurance, or whatever we're supposed to call it. I took the prescription to another brick-and-mortar eyeglass store in town and spent another $400 or $500 on two pairs of frames and lenses.</p>

<p>The glasses were great. Being able to see clearly was fantastic. The bill was not.</p>

<p>Since then, I have learned two very important things.</p>

<p>First, the quality of the person examining your eyes matters—a lot.</p>

<p>Second, you do not necessarily need to buy your glasses from the same place that examines your eyes. In fact, based on my recent experience, I may never buy prescription glasses from a traditional brick-and-mortar eyeglass store again.</p>

<h2>Fast and Available Isn't Always Better</h2>

<p>About a month ago, I needed another eye exam. I called around, but the places with the best reputations couldn't see me for nearly a month. Another eye center could get me in almost immediately, so I went there.</p>

<p>I'm not going to name the place because I'm not trying to trash anybody's business. I'm simply going to explain what happened.</p>

<p>The entire process felt like I was being herded through as quickly as possible.</p>

<p>Get in here. Look at this. Blink. Look over there. Tell me what you see. Move to the next machine. Now go into this room.</p>

<p>Several of the people running me through the tests didn't appear to be optometrists or medical assistants. They seemed to be regular front-desk employees who had been trained to operate the equipment. Then, during the final part of the exam, the optometrist wasn't physically in the room. The optometrist appeared on a television screen and remotely controlled the machine while asking, "Which is better—one or two? Two or three? Three or four?"</p>

<p>I don't have a problem with technology. I make my living with technology. I also don't believe that a remote optometrist automatically means you're receiving bad care.</p>

<p><strong>The problem was that everything felt rushed.</strong></p>

<p>If I hesitated because I wasn't sure which lens looked clearer, I felt like I was holding up the assembly line. There was no time to slow down, look again and be certain. They seemed far more interested in getting me through the process than getting to know me or making sure the prescription was right.</p>

<p>They gave me trial contact lenses, and almost immediately I thought, "These things don't work."</p>

<p>At first, I wondered whether I simply needed time to adjust. I had never worn contacts before, so maybe they were supposed to feel strange. Maybe my eyes needed a week or two to get used to them.</p>

<p>After trying them for a couple of weeks, I knew that wasn't the problem. The prescription was wrong.</p>

<h2>Then Came the $1,200 Quote</h2>

<p>Let's go back to the first eye center for a minute.</p>

<p>After my exam, I planned to have new prescription lenses installed in two nice frames I already owned. I wasn't buying frames. I only needed lenses.</p>

<p>When we reached the checkout desk, the person told me they had an amazing promotion going on. There was a discount—20% off, if I remember correctly—and they could put new lenses in both of my existing frames for the bargain price of approximately <strong>$1,200</strong>.</p>

<p>That was my "stop the buggy" moment.</p>

<p><strong>Pump the brakes. We are not going any further.</strong></p>

<p>I told them to give me my prescription and that I would figure out the rest myself.</p>

<p>By the way, you are entitled to receive your eyeglass prescription after completing an eye exam without being forced to buy glasses from that office. That isn't a favor. It is required by the Federal Trade Commission's <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/eyeglass-rule" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Eyeglass Rule</a>. Contact-lens prescriptions must also be provided after the contact-lens fitting is completed under the FTC's <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/contact-lens-rule" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Contact Lens Rule</a>.</p>

<p><strong>Take your prescription. It belongs to you.</strong></p>

<h2>Check the Price of Vision Insurance</h2>

<p>The first time I bought glasses, I didn't have vision insurance. I didn't realize how inexpensive it could be or how much it might save me.</p>

<p>My current plan takes $150 off the price of frames and covers the lenses, including some upgraded lens options. The exact benefits will obviously depend on your plan, so look at the annual cost, what it covers, which retailers accept it and whether you are limited to one frame allowance per year.</p>

<p>Don't blindly buy insurance because I said it saved me money. Do the math based on your own plan. In my case, however, the savings were substantial.</p>

<p>Clemson Eye has an optical shop inside its facility, although it is operated by another company. Unfortunately, that optical provider didn't accept my particular vision insurance. That sent me online to see what other options were available.</p>

<p>I'm glad it did.</p>

<h2>What You Need Before Ordering Online</h2>

<p>Buying online is easy, but you need to pay attention to a few things.</p>

<p>Make sure your prescription is correct before ordering anything. If the prescription feels wrong, don't spend hundreds of dollars hoping your eyes will eventually adjust. Call the doctor and have it checked.</p>

<p>You will also need your pupillary distance, usually called your <strong>PD</strong>. That is the distance between your pupils, and it helps the company position the optical center of each lens correctly.</p>

<p>Pay attention to the measurements printed inside a pair of glasses that already fits you well. Those numbers usually tell you the lens width, bridge width and temple length. Online stores often provide virtual try-on tools, but actual measurements are more reliable than guessing from a picture.</p>

<p>Finally, check the return, exchange and prescription-remake policies before ordering. Glasses can look great on a website and fit differently once they arrive. A good return policy matters—especially the first time you order online.</p>

<p>If you have a particularly complicated prescription or need specialized lenses, you may still benefit from working directly with an experienced optician. I'm not claiming that every physical eyeglass store is a rip-off or that buying online is perfect for every person.</p>

<p>I'm saying you should compare your options before automatically handing over your credit card.</p>

<h2>The Real Lesson</h2>

<p><strong>Spend your money on getting the eye exam right.</strong></p>

<p>Find a reputable optometrist who listens to you, gives you time to compare the lenses and cares whether you can actually see. If the best doctor requires you to wait a month, wait a month. Your eyes are too important to choose a provider based entirely on who has an opening this afternoon.</p>

<p>Once you have an accurate prescription, shop around.</p>

<p>Check your vision-insurance benefits. Compare the in-house optical shop with reputable online retailers. Know your frame measurements. Understand what lens upgrades you actually need. Look at the complete price after insurance, taxes and shipping.</p>

<p>Most importantly, remember that the person who examines your eyes and the company that sells you glasses do not have to be the same business.</p>

<p>I learned that lesson the expensive way the first time. Fortunately, I hit the brakes before learning it again with a $1,200 bill.</p>
